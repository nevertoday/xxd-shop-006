# XXD Shop 006 · Original brief (English reading translation)

> Reading translation only. The canonical runtime prompt is [zh-CN.md](zh-CN.md).

From the garment or fabric product images and written description supplied by the user, generate a professional fabric-material showcase visual.

First identify the product and its materials.

Carefully read the fabric name, composition, process, structure, function and selling-point text provided by the user, and observe in the product images its weave, yarn, pile, pores, thickness, sheen, drape and surface structure.

If you meet content that is unfamiliar, uncertain in meaning, possibly an industry term, a brand technology name, a new material, a yarn name, a finishing process or a special composite structure, do not guess from the literal words. First search reliable sources online — preferably official brand material, manufacturers' technical notes, textile-industry resources, standards bodies or professional literature — to confirm whether it is:

a fibre raw material,
a yarn structure,
a weaving method,
a knit structure,
a composite structure,
a surface treatment,
a finishing process,
a functional technology,
or simply a commercial name.

Further confirm what appearance, microstructure, hand feel, thickness, elasticity, breathability, moisture absorption, warmth, water repellency or other characteristics this material usually has in reality.

Distinguish "properties of the material itself", "effects a specific process may bring" and "selling points in brand promotion"; unverified marketing descriptions must not be visualised directly as scientific fact.

Only after this understanding decide the visual expression best suited to the product, rather than applying a fixed fabric template.

If the core advantage comes from yarn and weave, focus on real fibres, stitches, structure and macro detail;

if it comes from softness, loft, drape or thickness, express touch through natural folding, rolling, pressing, layering and side light;

if it comes from mesh, breathability or moisture-wicking structure, focus on pores, air paths and the real fabric structure, with functional graphics only as minimal support;

if it comes from water repellency or surface treatment, express it through believable droplet states, surface tension and material detail, never creating effects inconsistent with the real material;

if it is a multi-layer laminate, padded, membrane or insulating structure, use a refined, real layered cross-section or partial exploded structure, giving each layer correct thickness and form;

if the selling point comes from cotton, wool, silk, plant fibre or another raw material, confirmed raw-material forms may build supporting imagery, but never replace the real fabric with a photo of the raw material.

Strictly follow the product images provided by the user, keeping the real colour, pattern, weave, texture scale and material character. The visual should let people feel "what this cloth really is", not fantasise ordinary cloth into another, more luxurious material.

Choose the composition as needed: macro, partial folds, flat lay, layering, cross-section, raw-material relationship or function demonstration. Keep the image clean and focused, showing the core material across a large area; the background actively recedes, and light carefully depicts fibre undulation, yarn edges, thickness, fuzz and surface microstructure.

If text is needed, use minimal professional infographic logic.
Label only information provided by the user or verified by sources, keep text as short as possible, and use a few thin lines, local pointers, hierarchy labels or functional symbols to aid understanding; never let text cover important texture, never turn the image into a manual.

The final goal is not "a pretty fabric picture", but to use the most suitable photographic, macro, structural or information-visual language to accurately express the material value that truly makes this specific product worth buying.
